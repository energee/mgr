# Entity Model & Permissions

> **Purpose.** Shared reference knowledge about the system's business record types (entities), how they relate to each other, and who is allowed to see or change them. Written for a general audience — safe to read as-is by engineering tooling today and by an in-product chat assistant later. This file is the source of truth for these rules: when a domain rule changes, update it here first.

## Entity inventory

The system manages 39 distinct types of business records — brands, batches, recipes, orders, purchase orders, inventory items, vessels, kegs, pricing tiers, customers, suppliers, and more. The most important relationships between them:

- A **brew log** records the brew day (mash through knockout). A **batch** is the fermentation-through-packaging record. One brew log can split into several batches, and several brew logs can blend into one batch — they are linked with a volume allocation for each link, not a strict one-to-one.
- An **order** belongs to a customer and has many **order items**, each naming a brand, selling format, and quantity (optionally tied to a specific batch or packaged product). Customers can submit **change requests** against their own pending orders; each change request has line-item-level add/modify/remove entries (also brand + selling format) and needs staff approval.
- A **purchase order** goes to a supplier and has many **PO line items** (one per ingredient/catalog item ordered); each line item can be received in multiple partial **receives**, and each receive can create a lot of raw-material inventory.
- A **packaging session** groups the packaging work for a given day and has many **session line items**, each one recording a brand + package format + the batches it was filled from, with planned vs. actual quantities. Packaging sessions consume materials through `selling_format_materials` — there is no direct link table between a session and a material; the bill-of-materials relationship goes through the selling format instead, by design.
- **Finished goods stock is ledger-style.** A finished-goods record's quantity means "how many were packaged," and it is never edited afterward by day-to-day sales flows. Every reservation or removal is a separate **allocation** entry (finished good → order, taproom sale, sample, loss, …), and available stock is always *derived* as packaged quantity minus open-plus-completed allocations. Fulfilling an order therefore completes its allocation entries — stamping when and how much volume left the brewery, which is what the TTB report counts as removals — and deliberately does **not** decrement the finished-goods quantity (doing both would subtract the same beer twice). Cancelling an order releases its still-planned allocations so the reserved stock becomes available again; anything already shipped stays recorded as removed.
- The **keg** cluster tracks physical keg fleet by owner (Owned, Microstar, third-party fleets): keg types have a per-owner deposit amount, keg transactions record fills/movements, and customer keg balances are derived from those transactions. Keg transactions also carry a **bin** dimension (which bin the kegs left / entered, parallel to the location legs): packaging fills stamp the filled kegs into a bin — the packaging session's default bin, else the location's default finished-goods bin — and both dimensions **net**: a revision that reduces a fill, and each shipped lot, record counter-legs in the same location/bin group as the fill they reverse, so the filled-keg count by brand (`keg_filled_contents`) reflects what is physically on hand. Filled kegs are deliberately **never** written to `bin_inventory` (that would double-count); their bin presence lives entirely on the keg transactions. The **owner** dimension nets by *fill attribution*: a ship leg records the **order line's** keg owner (that value feeds customer keg balances and per-owner deposits), but fleet counts (`keg_inventory`) attribute every filled-state leg that names a finished good to the owner that finished good was **filled** under (the packaging session line's owner) — otherwise a keg filled without an owner but shipped under a named owner would inflate the fleet forever, because the ship's negative leg lands in an owner group no fill ever fed and netting discards it.
- **Bins are the point-of-sale targets.** A bin (not a location) is a Square POS sync target when it carries both a Square location mapping (`bins.square_location_id`, at most one bin per Square location) and a POS sales channel (`bins.pos_sales_channel_id`). The unified `sellable_inventory` view — packaged finished goods in bins UNION filled-keg contents by bin — is what the Square catalog and inventory syncs read per POS bin. An inbound Square sale resolves its Square location back to that bin and draws the sold quantity oldest-first across the finished-good lots physically in it; draft (keg) pours are staged for reconciliation rather than depleting kegs automatically. The sale/refund claim, every allocation, bin debit/credit, draft mutation, and claim finalization commit in one database transaction. Sales deduplicate per Square **order** (one check can arrive as several payment events or split tenders), refunds deduplicate per Square refund, and sale/refund transactions for one order serialize. Separate partial refunds calculate a cumulative target for every original sale allocation and apply only the unreversed delta, so integer rounding catches up as the order's refunded amount grows and can never credit past the sold quantity. A failed transaction leaves no partial inventory effect and is safe to retry; an incomplete claim created before the atomic flow is never replayed because its earlier effects are unknowable, and is instead marked for manual reconciliation.
- **Stateful records always enter their lifecycle at the start.** Entities with a status workflow (batches, orders, purchase orders, packaging sessions, brew logs, pick lists, allocations, recipes, vessels, yeast pitches, user profiles, deliveries, location transfers) can only be *created* in their initial status ("Planned", "Draft", …); every later status is reached exclusively through an explicit transition. That is deliberate: transitions are what run the attached side effects (a packaging session reaching "Completed" is what consumes packaging materials and creates finished goods; an order reaching "Fulfilled" is what completes its stock reservations and stamps the TTB removal) and what the database's transition triggers validate — a record born directly in a later status would skip all of it. Create forms therefore offer only the initial status and force it on save, and a status value outside an entity's state machine is rejected by validation everywhere (forms, quick-create, services, AI writes).
- **Distributor spreadsheet orders have explicit source ownership.** `Beer orders.xlsx` is reconciled through Settings → Integrations; MongoDB is never an order source. Every upload is previewed and hashed before an explicit atomic apply. New orders enter at `draft`, existing lifecycle statuses are preserved, deterministic line IDs are updated in place, and orders absent from a later workbook are reported but never automatically deleted. In House/taproom blocks are skipped because internal stock is reserved through bins and allocations rather than customer orders; external keg lines use Microstar ownership and workbook Distributor-tier prices.
- The **pricing tier** cluster is a matrix: each pricing tier (e.g. "Tier 1") has a price for every combination of package format and sales channel; every price change is written to an audit history automatically.
  - **Two pricing paths, by design (dual model).** *Wholesale* order pricing is resolved from the **customer's** tier: `get_price_for_customer(customer, format, …)` reads the customer's `price_tier_id` + `sales_channel_id` and returns the matching `tier × format × channel` cell. It ignores brand/style/effective-date (the temporal and brand-specific dimensions were dropped in the 00077 redesign); `is_brand_specific`/`is_style_specific` are always `false`. *Taproom / Square* pricing instead uses the **product's** tier — `recipes.pricing_tier_id` (a COGS-band assignment) resolved by the Square sync to the matrix cell of **each POS bin's own sales channel** (`bins.pos_sales_channel_id`): every POS-configured bin names the channel whose prices its stock is pushed at, so different bins can sell at different channels' prices (v1 pushes a single price per Square variation — a variation stocked at bins on different channels deterministically takes the first bin's channel). Same matrix, two different tier selectors; the two paths are separate on purpose, so the same brand can carry a wholesale price and a POS price that are chosen by different rules.
  - **There is no "default tier per channel."** `get_price_for_customer` returns *nothing* unless the customer has **both** a `price_tier_id` and a `sales_channel_id` set — so a customer missing either simply gets no auto-suggested price (staff enter it manually). A customer must be given a tier + channel before auto-pricing (or a portal invite) is meaningful; the sales channel does not imply a default tier.

## Permission model

Every user has one or more roles — admin, production manager, brewer, sales, viewer, or customer — and what a role can see or change is defined by named permissions (e.g. "batches: view," "orders: edit") rather than the role name itself, so access can be tuned per area of the product (recipes, batches, orders, customers, inventory, purchasing, vessels, integrations, settings). Admins can do everything. Most staff roles can view broadly within their area and edit only what their role covers (e.g. brewers can edit batches and recipes but not orders; sales can edit orders and customers but not inventory). Shared reference data (brands, ingredient catalogs, pricing tiers) is viewable by any signed-in staff member but only editable by admins. Customers only see their own orders, their own change requests, and their own keg balances — never other customers' data or internal operational records. The product has a single organization — there is no multi-company or multi-location data separation; permission scoping is entirely role-based.

Role assignment is invite-only — there is no self-serve signup. Staff are invited by an admin (who picks their roles); customer contacts are invited from a customer's Portal Access section. A customer can have multiple independently authenticated contacts, and one contact can be linked to multiple customers through `customer_portal_users`. When a new auth user's email case-insensitively matches an active customer's primary email, the profile trigger assigns the customer role (the very first profile ever created becomes admin; anyone else without a customer match defaults to viewer). Additional-contact emails do not have to match the primary email, so the customer-invite route provisions the auth identity without sending mail, replaces only the untouched viewer default with the customer role, writes and verifies the junction link, and only then sends a usable OTP email; an existing staff role is never overwritten. Invite success requires the returned profile roles/status and portal link to match the requested authorization. If a newly created Auth identity cannot be fully provisioned or emailed, the route deletes it as compensation; a cleanup failure is surfaced for operator repair rather than reported as success. A user whose roles include customer is always routed to the portal, never the staff app. The customer role carries an empty staff permission set — portal access works through customer-scoped row policies, not staff permissions. Broadcast notifications (order/batch/packaging events) go only to active staff profiles, never to customer-role users, because their text can embed other customers' names and order numbers.
